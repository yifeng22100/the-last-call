When should a bank stop calling?

A Portuguese bank phoned 41,188 clients 105,754 times between 2008 and 2010 to sell term deposits (Moro, Rita, & Cortez, 2014). Most analyses of this data ask who will say yes. A call centre needs to know something else: is the next call to this person worth making?

Three traps in common analyses of this dataset

It is a widely used teaching dataset with many public notebooks on Kaggle and GitHub. Before building anything, I reproduced the usual approach and stress-tested it. The original authors warned about two of these problems (Moro, Cortez, & Rita, 2014), yet many public notebooks still fall into them.

More calls don't convert worse. The counting does.

Group clients by how many times they were called and conversion seems to fall off a cliff after the first call. That chart double-counts: anyone who says yes on call 1 is never called again, so the "called 3 times" group contains only people who already said no twice. Measured correctly, as the success rate of each call among the people still being called, the second call is as productive as the first. A causal-inference tutorial on this dataset makes the same point (The effects of marketing decisions, n.d.).

What I built instead

A next-call model. Each client is expanded into one row per call, so the model estimates the chance that the next call succeeds, given who the client is, how many times they've been called and the economic context. This is a discrete-time hazard model (Singer & Willett, 1993), fitted with scikit-learn (Pedregosa et al., 2011). It only uses information known before the call: no duration, no total call count, no timing of the last contact.

Tested the way it would run. Every month from March 2009 is predicted by a model trained only on earlier months. Four candidate models were declared up front and the winner was picked on 2009 alone, with 2010 held back. The pick was plain logistic regression. Gradient boosting came last when given the economic indicators (it kept of 2009 subscriptions at half the calls, against for logistic regression) and did fine without them (). Tree models can't extrapolate past the interest rates seen in training, and 2009 rates were far below anything in 2008.

A stopping rule priced in money. Keep calling while the predicted chance of a yes is at least the cost of a call divided by the value of a new deposit. That rule is only safe if the probabilities are honest, so the model runs behind three safeguards, described in the pilot section below.

Ready for a pilot

Here is how the rule would have behaved if it had gone live in March 2009, using only information available at the start of each month.

  • Shadow mode first. In its first live month the model scores every call but cuts none, and predictions are compared with outcomes.
  • Monthly recalibration. Each month the predictions are shifted so that last month's would have matched last month's real success rate, a standard update known as recalibration-in-the-large (Steyerberg, 2019).
  • Drift alarm. If last month's odds were off by more than 3×, the model is not trusted: back to shadow mode, every call is made, and an analyst reviews it.

Why this matters: in March 2009, right after the rate collapse, the model trained on 2008 gave of that month's calls less than a 1% chance, and of them succeeded. No model trained before a regime break can know the new level. The safeguards catch that, and with them the calls the rule cuts succeed at about the rate the model predicted ( observed at a 5% threshold). I designed these safeguards after seeing the 2009 to 2010 results, so for this part those years are not a clean test. The pilot is.

Pilot plan

The obvious pilot, randomising clients to the old process or the rule and comparing net value per client, needs an impractically large sample unless calls are expensive relative to deposits, because the rule's gain per client is small against the noise. A sharper test goes straight at the decision. Calls the rule would cut are worth cutting only if their success rate is below break-even (cost ÷ value). So make a random half of those flagged calls anyway and test whether they succeed less often than break-even.

  1. Weeks 1 to 2: shadow. Score every scheduled call and change nothing. Check that predictions match outcomes before acting.
  2. From week 3: live, with a holdback. The rule flags calls below break-even. Half of the flagged calls, chosen at random, are made anyway. Primary test: is their success rate below break-even? (One-sided, 5% significance, 80% power.) Guardrails: complaints, opt-outs, and the age check below.
  3. Launch decision. Cut flagged calls fully once the test concludes. Keep a small holdback, the drift alarm and monthly recalibration in production as ongoing monitoring.

Limits

  • One bank, one product, 2008 to 2010. The public file is a subset of the authors' 2008 to 2013 data (52,944 contacts, 150 features), so the results here are not comparable like-for-like with the paper's.
  • The data is observational. The rule estimates what the next call is likely to yield; it does not prove that calling causes the yes. Only the randomised pilot can show that.
  • The one-row-per-call expansion assumes the bank stops calling a client once they subscribe, and that client details and channel are the same on every call of a campaign.
  • On the 2010 holdout, the next-call model and a plain client-scoring model perform the same at half the calls. Its advantage there is the money-priced stopping rule, not better ranking.
  • The economic indicators are those at the time of contact. Some are published quarterly with a delay, so a live system would use the latest available value.
